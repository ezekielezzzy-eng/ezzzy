What Software Engineers Get Wrong About Writing

Thomas Vael

August 14, 2026 · 5 min read

Code and prose share a surprising number of virtues. Clarity, precision, economy. But engineers who turn to writing tend to apply the wrong lessons from their technical practice.

The first mistake is treating writing like debugging. Engineers are trained to hunt errors, to find the thing that is wrong and fix it. Applied to a first draft, this produces paralysis. The first draft is supposed to be wrong. Its job is to exist, not to work.

The second mistake is optimizing for the reader's time. In software, efficiency is nearly always a virtue. In prose, it depends. A sentence that can be read quickly is not always better than one that makes you slow down. Sometimes the right sentence is the one that stops you.

The third mistake is mistaking correctness for quality. A program that compiles is not necessarily a good program. An essay with no grammatical errors is not necessarily a good essay. The standards are different, and the engineer's instinct to check whether something is right before evaluating whether it is good leads to a kind of premature optimization of the wrong variable.

What engineers do bring, genuinely, is a comfort with revision. The habit of treating the first version as a draft to be improved, the willingness to throw out a function that works but doesn't fit — these translate beautifully to writing.

The best technical writers I know are engineers who learned to distrust their first draft without hating it. They fix what's broken without confusing "broken" with "not yet finished."
